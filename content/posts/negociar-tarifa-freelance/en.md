During my early years as a freelancer, my rate was decided by the discomfort of the moment. I'd say a number, watch the other person's face, and if it looked too high I'd lower it in the same conversation. That isn't negotiating a freelance rate. It's letting someone else's reaction set my price.

## Why "charge what you're worth" isn't a method

It's real advice and useless without a calculation behind it. "What you're worth" isn't a number that shows up on its own — it comes from adding up concrete things, and without that addition, the phrase is just a fancier way of saying "a number that feels right".

## Negotiating a freelance rate: what I add up first

**The real time, not the optimistic time.** If I estimate two weeks, I calculate the rate over three. Not out of pessimism: in years of projects, almost none went as estimated at the start. It is the [planning fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy) by the book, and charging on the optimistic time means giving away the extra hours.

**The risk I'm absorbing, not the client.** A project with defined scope and payment against milestones carries less risk than one with ambiguous scope and payment only at the end. The second costs more: the risk of it stretching out unpaid is mine.

**The value my combination of skills brings.** I don't charge the same for work any developer could do as for work where designing the UX/UI myself removes an entire round-trip. That combination is worth concrete time saved for the client, not just "more experience". I went into it in [a full stack developer or a team](/en/blog/solo-full-stack-developer-or-a-team-when-each-makes-sense).

**What I give up being able to accept by taking this project.** A month booked with one client is a month I can't spend on another. That opportunity cost is real even though it never shows up on an invoice.

## How I communicate the number

I say the number once, with the breakdown behind it if asked, and I don't change it out of discomfort. If someone questions it, the answer isn't lowering the price on the spot. It's explaining again what it includes, or asking which part of the scope they want adjusted, so the number changes for a reason.

Lowering the price because someone made a surprised face is the fastest way to teach a client the original number was never serious.

## The opposite mistake, which I've also made

It isn't only charging too little. I've also overpriced a couple of times, calculating the risk of an ambiguous project without making it explicit. I ended up asking a high number I couldn't explain. A price I can't justify with a breakdown is as fragile as one that's too low. The difference is it only shows once someone asks, and by then it has already generated distrust.

## The sign the number is well calculated

I can explain every part without hesitation, on the spot, without inventing a justification on the fly. If my own question of "why this number and not another?" surprises me, that's a sign I didn't calculate it: I felt it.

The step before all of this is knowing whether the project is worth taking. Looking at it from the other side of the table helps, with the same [questions someone should ask before hiring a freelance developer](/en/blog/questions-to-ask-before-hiring-a-freelance-developer).

## What I do when the client's budget falls short

I don't lower the rate: I lower the scope. They are different things and confusing them ruins both sides. If the budget covers sixty per cent of the project, I propose the sixty per cent that solves the main problem and leave the rest documented as a second stage.

That way the client gets something that works and I get paid for the work I actually did. Nobody ends up with a whole version done halfway.
