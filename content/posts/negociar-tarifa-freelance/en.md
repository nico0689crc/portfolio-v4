During my early years as a freelancer, my rate was decided by the discomfort of the moment: I'd say a number, watch the other person's face, and if it looked too high, I'd lower it in the same conversation. That isn't negotiating. It's letting someone else's reaction set my price.

## Why "charge what you're worth" isn't a method

It's real advice and useless without a calculation behind it. "What you're worth" isn't a number that shows up on its own — it comes from adding up concrete things, and without that addition, the phrase is just a fancier way of saying "a number that feels right".

## What I add up before giving any number

**The real time, not the optimistic time.** If I estimate two weeks, I calculate the rate over three. Not out of pessimism — because in years of projects, almost none went exactly as estimated at the start, and charging on the optimistic time means working the extra hours for free.

**The risk I'm absorbing, not the client.** A project with well-defined scope and payment against verifiable milestones carries less risk than one with ambiguous scope and payment only at the end. The second type of project costs more, because the risk of it stretching out without me getting paid for that is mine, not the client's.

**The specific value my combination of skills brings.** I don't charge the same for work any developer could do as for work where the fact that I also design UX/UI eliminates an entire round-trip with another person. That combination has a concrete, measurable value in time saved for the client, it isn't just "more experience".

**What I give up being able to accept by taking this project.** A month booked with one client is a month I can't spend on another. That opportunity cost is real even though it never shows up on an invoice.

## How I communicate the number

I say the number once, with the breakdown behind it if asked, and I don't change it in the same conversation out of discomfort. If someone questions it, the answer isn't lowering the price on the spot — it's explaining again what it includes, or asking specifically which part of the scope they want adjusted so the number changes for a reason, not without one.

Lowering the price because someone made a surprised face is the fastest way to teach a client the original number was never serious.

## The opposite mistake, which I've also made

It isn't only charging too little. I've also overpriced, a couple of times, calculating the risk of an ambiguous project without making it explicit, and ended up charging a high number I couldn't explain if someone asked. A price I can't justify with a breakdown is just as fragile as one that's too low — the difference is it only shows once someone asks, and by then it's already generated distrust.

## The sign the number is well calculated

I can explain every part of it without hesitation, on the spot, without having to invent a justification on the fly. If my own question of "why this number and not another?" surprises me, that's a sign I didn't calculate it — I felt it.
