A twelve-month roadmap, with specific features assigned to each quarter, looks impressive in a presentation. It's also, for most products that don't already have a fully validated market, a carefully designed fiction — a way of projecting control over something that actually depends on data that doesn't exist yet.

## Why the long horizon is a promise that can't be kept

To know what to build in month ten, I need to know how users responded to what I built in month three, what feedback changed my understanding of the problem, and what new opportunity appeared that I couldn't have anticipated today. A twelve-month roadmap has to pretend it already knows all those answers, when in reality none of them exist yet.

This isn't a bad-planning problem. It's a structural property of any product still learning from its market — which is the situation of almost every small and mid-sized product, including any project in its first year.

## What I do plan with a three-month horizon

Three months is short enough to avoid too much guessing and long enough for real continuity of work. In that window I can commit to specific features, because the context behind them — what problem they solve, what evidence justifies them — stays valid within that timeframe without having changed significantly.

I communicate that short roadmap with the same level of specificity others use for a year-long one: approximate dates, defined scope, clear success criteria. The difference isn't the plan's rigor, it's its honesty about how far what can actually be promised really reaches.

## What I explicitly leave as "direction, not commitment"

Beyond three months, I communicate general direction — where the product is heading, what bigger problem we're trying to solve — without committing to specific features or dates. This isn't evasion. It's the difference between saying "we're going to improve the payment experience" (direction) and "in month eight we're launching recurring payments with three different providers" (a commitment I have no way of backing with evidence today).

## How this relates to MVP scope

It's the same principle I applied when [deciding what to build in an MVP](/en/blog/from-idea-to-mvp-what-to-build): the discipline of explicitly saying what's left out isn't only for the first version, it's for every planning window. A three-month roadmap with a clear list of what doesn't fit in that period is more useful than a twelve-month one with everything included but nothing genuinely committed.

## The cost of communicating it this way, and why I accept it anyway

A short roadmap raises an uncomfortable question with investors or clients used to seeing year-long plans: "and after these three months, then what?". The honest answer — "it depends on what we learn in these three months" — sounds less confident than a detailed plan through December. But it's the true answer, and a twelve-month plan that gets completely rewritten by month four damages trust more than having been honest about the uncertainty from the start.

## The rule I apply

I plan in detail only what I can back with real evidence today, and I communicate the rest as direction open to change. Preferring a short, honest plan over a long, optimistic one isn't a lack of ambition — it's recognizing that ambition without evidence is a bet, and bets get communicated as such, not as certainties.
