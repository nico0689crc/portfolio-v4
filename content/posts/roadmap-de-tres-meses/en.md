A twelve-month product roadmap, with specific features assigned to each quarter, looks impressive in a presentation. For most products without a validated market, it's also a carefully designed fiction. A way of projecting control over something that depends on data that doesn't exist yet.

## Why the long horizon is a promise that can't be kept

To know what to build in month ten, I need to know how users responded to month three. What feedback changed my understanding of the problem, and what opportunity appeared that I can't anticipate today. A twelve-month roadmap pretends I already have those answers, when none of them exist yet.

This isn't bad planning. It's a structural property of any product still learning from its market, which is the situation of almost every small product, including any project in its first year. [Outcome-based roadmaps](https://www.productplan.com/learn/outcome-based-roadmap/) exist for exactly this reason.

## What goes into a three-month product roadmap

Three months is short enough to avoid too much guessing and long enough for real continuity of work. In that window I can commit to specific features. The context behind them, what problem they solve and what evidence justifies them, stays valid across that timeframe.

I communicate that short roadmap with the same specificity others use for a year-long one: approximate dates, defined scope, clear success criteria. The difference isn't the plan's rigor. It's its honesty about how far a promise can actually reach.

## What I explicitly leave as "direction, not commitment"

Beyond three months I communicate general direction: where the product is heading and what bigger problem we're trying to solve. Without committing to features or dates. This isn't evasion. It's the difference between "we're going to improve the payment experience" and "in month eight we're launching recurring payments with three providers", which I can't back with evidence today.

## How this relates to MVP scope

It's the same principle I applied when [deciding what to build in an MVP](/en/blog/from-idea-to-mvp-what-to-build). The discipline of explicitly saying what's left out isn't only for the first version: it's for every planning window. A three-month roadmap with a clear list of what doesn't fit beats a twelve-month one with everything included and nothing committed.

## The cost of communicating it this way, and why I accept it anyway

A short roadmap raises an uncomfortable question with clients used to year-long plans: "and after these three months, then what?". The honest answer, "it depends on what we learn", sounds less confident than a detailed plan through December. But it's the true one. A twelve-month plan rewritten by month four damages trust more than honesty about uncertainty from the start.

## The rule I apply

I plan in detail only what I can back with evidence today, and I communicate the rest as direction open to change. Preferring a short, honest plan over a long, optimistic one isn't a lack of ambition. It's recognizing that ambition without evidence is a bet, and bets get communicated as such.

## What I do at the end of each window

Before drawing up the next three months, I reread the previous three and note what landed, what didn't and why. It takes half an hour and it's what stops the next roadmap from repeating the same optimism.

I almost always find the same thing: I didn't fail at estimating the work, I failed at counting the work I never wrote down. Support, reviews, decisions that took longer than expected. That invisible margin is what eats the last two weeks of any plan. Writing it down once turns it from a recurring surprise into a line item the next window can actually budget for.
