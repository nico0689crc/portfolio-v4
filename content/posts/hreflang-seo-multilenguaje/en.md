Of every technical SEO mistake I've fixed on multilingual sites, badly declared hreflang is the one with the most disproportionate effect relative to how simple the mistake looks. One tag with the wrong URL can make Google drop from indexing not just that page, but the entire language cluster connected to it.

## What hreflang is, without the jargon

It's the tag that tells a search engine "this page has an equivalent version in another language, and it's at this URL". It's how Google shows a user in Spain your Spanish version and one in Germany your English version. Without it, both get the same URL regardless of language. [Google documents it in detail](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions).

## Why this hreflang SEO mistake is so common

The most frequent mistake is declaring `hreflang="en"` pointing to a URL that doesn't exist yet in English. A blog post only published in Spanish, say. It sounds harmless: "once I translate it, the tag will already be there". But in the meantime that tag tells Google "there's an English version here". Google goes to verify it, finds a 404, and the signal it receives isn't "doesn't exist yet": it's "this hreflang declaration isn't trustworthy".

And once a hreflang declaration isn't trustworthy on one page, Google tends to distrust the whole cluster, not just that specific tag. The cost isn't proportional to the size of the mistake.

## The criteria I apply: only declare what exists

The rule I follow, no exceptions: a language gets declared in hreflang only once that translation is already published, with `status = published`. Not when it's "coming soon", and not when it exists as a draft. On this site, each post's hreflang is generated by querying which translations are published at that moment. If the English version is still a draft, the Spanish note's hreflang doesn't mention English.

This means a post just published in one language doesn't announce the other until the other genuinely exists. It's less ambitious than declaring both from day one, and it's the only version that doesn't lie to Google.

## The second mistake, less common but just as damaging

Declaring reciprocal hreflang inconsistently. The Spanish version points correctly to the English one, but the English one doesn't point back, or points elsewhere. Google treats the relationship as bidirectional: if A says B is its pair, B has to say the same. If they don't match, the whole signal gets discarded.

The way to avoid this isn't reviewing every page by hand. It's generating both directions from the same data source, so it's structurally impossible for them to fall out of sync. If hreflang gets built by reading which slugs are published in each language for the same internal key, both sides always match.

## Why `x-default` matters too

Besides per-language hreflang, you need to declare which version shows to someone whose language doesn't match any of the declared ones. Without `x-default`, that visitor gets unspecified behaviour that varies between search engines. With it, the decision is explicit and consistent.

On a bilingual site the choice is usually the language most of your traffic already reads, or the one whose content is most complete. Here it points to the Spanish version, because that is where every post exists first and where a translation is never missing.

## The check I do after publishing

I don't trust the implementation is correct just because the code looks right. I check in Search Console that no hreflang errors are reported for recently published pages. It's the only way to confirm that what Google reads matches what the code generates, instead of assuming they match because they should.
