No budget breaks with one big announcement. It breaks with fifteen small decisions that, each on its own, seem reasonable to accept. The trick isn't avoiding all of them — some are necessary — it's learning to see them while they can still be discussed, not after they've already piled up.

## The signals I've learned to watch for

**The word "also" starts showing up often.** "And we'd also need..." is the phrase that most often precedes a new scope request. It isn't a problem for it to appear — that's normal, projects evolve. The problem is when it appears without anyone noticing it's piling up, because each individual "also" sounds like a small ask.

**Follow-up meetings get longer instead of shorter.** If by week eight the meetings run longer than in week two, it's usually because there are more unresolved decisions than there were at the start, not fewer. The project should be getting clearer over time, not more complicated.

**Someone says "while we're at it".** It's the most dangerous phrase in a custom project, because it sounds like efficiency — taking advantage of already touching something to add one more thing — and it almost always hides a whole new feature disguised as a minor tweak.

**The original critical path stops being the first thing demoed.** If your follow-up demos stopped showing the main flow — the one that answers the original business question — and started showing secondary features instead, that's a sign focus has shifted without anyone explicitly deciding so.

## Why it's worth saying as soon as you notice it, not at the end

Saying "this is going to cost more than budgeted" in week three, about one concrete request, is a five-minute conversation. Saying it in week fourteen, about the accumulation of ten requests nobody added up in time, is an uncomfortable conversation about why nobody spoke up sooner — and that second conversation damages trust far more than the first.

The rule I apply: every time something gets added to scope, I name it in the moment, I don't let it pass thinking "it's small". A small request is still a request, and the client has the right to decide whether to pay for it now or leave it for later, not to find out retroactively that they already paid for it without ever deciding to.

## How I communicate it without it sounding like an excuse

I don't say "this wasn't in scope" as a complaint. I say specifically what changed and what the options are: "This adds roughly three days of work. We can add it now, adjusting the timeline, or note it for a second stage." That turns a red flag into a decision the client makes with full information, instead of a surprise they discover later.

## The other side: when the extra budget is nobody's fault

Not every one of these situations comes from a client asking for more. Sometimes the underestimate is mine: a feature that sounded simple in the plan turns out to have technical complexity that wasn't visible from outside. The conversation there is the same — say it as soon as it's noticed, with the concrete option on the table — but the responsibility for having estimated wrong is mine, and I say so plainly, without dancing around it.

## What this has to do with [how I wrote about choosing what to build](/en/blog/from-idea-to-mvp-what-to-build)

The explicit list of what's left out of scope isn't only a tool for starting a project. It's the reference I use throughout the whole project to detect, with evidence and not with a gut feeling, when something being requested genuinely fell outside the original agreement. Without that written list, every new request gets evaluated from memory, and memory is exactly where scope leaks in without anyone noticing.
