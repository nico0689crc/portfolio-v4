Getting freelance clients through platforms solves a real problem: they connect you with demand that would otherwise be hard to find. The problem isn't that they exist. It's depending on them exclusively, because that dependency has a cost that grows over time instead of shrinking.

## Why depending on platforms for getting freelance clients is a risk

The commission a platform charges is the visible cost and the least important one. The real cost is different. Every client who arrives through a platform saw you after seeing all your competitors in the same search. They compare price before comparing judgement, with no context about you beyond a generic profile. It's the hardest possible format in which to land a client who values anything beyond price, because the format itself pushes everyone to compete on price.

## What I build instead, in parallel, not as an instant replacement

**Content that demonstrates judgement, not just skill.** This very blog is an example of the principle. Every article that explains a real decision — why I chose a stack, how I prioritized a UX problem — is evidence of how I think. It's available to anyone who looks for it, without me having to be present in the conversation. A client who arrives having read two or three articles already knows how I work before the first meeting. That's exactly the conversation a platform never allows you to have.

**Real case studies, not just a portfolio of screenshots.** I wrote separately about [how I choose which projects go in my portfolio](/en/blog/how-i-choose-which-projects-go-in-my-portfolio). Showing a nice picture of a finished project isn't the same as explaining the whole process behind it — the problem, the decisions, the numbers. It's the difference between looking capable and demonstrating how you think through a project end to end.

**Relationships that survive a single project.** A satisfied client who refers you to another is the cheapest, most reliable way to get new work. It doesn't exist in the platform model, where every project is an isolated transaction with no memory of the last one. Keeping in touch after a project ends, even occasionally, turns a client into a recurring source instead of a one-off payment.

**Presence where the people I need are already looking for information, not clients.** Participating in technical or product communities — from [Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/) to smaller groups in your niche — and answering real questions with no immediate sales intent builds a reputation that later turns into opportunities. But only if the participation is genuine and not a disguised pitch.

## Why this isn't a criticism of using platforms

Platforms are still useful, especially at the start, when there's no reputation built yet. The mistake isn't using them. It's not building anything in parallel that reduces the dependency over time, so that five years later you're as dependent on that platform as you were on day one.

## The time horizon this requires

Nothing I've mentioned produces a client the week you start. Writing content, building case studies, maintaining relationships: all of that is an investment that pays off in months, not days. That's exactly why many freelancers never do it. The platform gives a faster result in the short term, even though it's worse in the long run.

It's worth saying without romanticizing it: sustaining that investment while billing through platforms is uncomfortable, because the time comes out of the same hours. The only approach that worked for me is treating it as a small recurring task, not a separate project that will start someday.

## The question I ask myself to decide where to invest time

Does what I'm doing right now leave me with something that keeps working for me after it's done? Writing an article keeps generating value months after it's published. Applying to a project on a platform ends its value the moment that project gets resolved, whether the proposal wins or loses. That difference decides where I put the time not already spent on billable work.
