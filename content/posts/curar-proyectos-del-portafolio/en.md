The temptation to show everything you've built is understandable: every project cost real time, and leaving it out feels like wasting that effort. But portfolio projects aren't a historical archive. They're a specific argument about what kind of work you can do, and every project that doesn't help that argument steals space from the ones that do.

## The question I apply to every candidate for the portfolio projects list

**Does this project demonstrate a type of decision I want associated with me?** Not "did it turn out well?" nor "did it take a lot of work?", but specifically whether it shows the kind of judgement I want a recruiter or a client to see as representative of how I work.

## What a project needs to have to get in

**A decision that can be explained with reasoning, not just a result.** The [Mexx case](/en/projects/mexx-ux-redesign) gets in because I can explain the whole process: why those two problems, why those six solutions and not the original ten, what the test revealed that I wasn't expecting. A project where I can only say "it turned out well" doesn't contribute the same thing, even if the visual result is just as good.

**Something verifiable, not just my word.** GymSmartAccess gets in because it's live in production, with real customers, and that can be verified by visiting the site. A project that only exists as screenshots of something no longer online carries less weight. Not because the work wasn't real, but because nobody can confirm it on their own.

**An honest lesson, not just an achievement.** I prefer a case that includes what went wrong and what got done about it — like the header misclick rate I documented unresolved at Mexx — over one that only tells wins. A portfolio that never shows a limit reads as incomplete, not as perfect.

## What I leave out, even if it took a lot of work

**Projects where I made no relevant decision.** Work executed following someone else's exact instructions, with no room for my own judgement, doesn't demonstrate what a portfolio needs to. Even if the work itself was solid.

**Projects that no longer reflect how I work today.** Something from several years ago, done with judgement I wouldn't apply anymore, subtracts more than it adds. It suggests that's still my current level, when it isn't.

**Projects redundant with one already included.** If two cases demonstrate exactly the same type of decision, one of the two is excess. I'd rather every project show a different facet — research, technical architecture, business decision — than repeat the same strength three times.

## The mistake I've seen most often in other people's portfolios

Including every project ever done, in chronological order, with no curation at all. That turns the portfolio into a résumé with pictures, not an argument. The person looking at it has to find what's important among ten similar-looking projects. That selection work should have been done by whoever built the portfolio.

## How I decide the order of the portfolio projects

The strongest project goes first, not the most recent one. Whoever looks at a portfolio rarely makes it to the end. Chronological order — which prioritizes the last thing I did — almost never matches the order that best communicates my judgement. It's the same logic as the [inverted pyramid](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/) used in writing for the web.

## The final question before publishing

If someone looks at only the first two projects and leaves, do they already have a clear idea of what kind of work I do? If the answer is no, the problem isn't that more projects are missing. It's that the first two aren't choosing well what they need to communicate.
