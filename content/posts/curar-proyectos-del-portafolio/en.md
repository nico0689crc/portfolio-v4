The temptation to show everything you've built is understandable — every project cost real time, and leaving it out feels like wasting that effort. But a portfolio isn't a historical archive. It's a specific argument about what kind of work you can do, and every project that doesn't help that argument steals space and attention from the ones that do.

## The question I apply to every candidate project

**Does this project demonstrate a type of decision I want associated with me?** Not "did it turn out well?" nor "did it take a lot of work?", but specifically whether the project shows the kind of judgement I want a recruiter or a client to see as representative of how I work.

## What a project needs to have to get in

**A decision that can be explained with reasoning, not just a result.** The Mexx case gets in because I can explain the whole process — why those two problems, why those six solutions and not the original ten, what the test revealed that I wasn't expecting. A project where I can only say "it turned out well" without being able to narrate the why doesn't contribute the same thing, even if the visual result is just as good.

**Something verifiable, not just my word.** GymSmartAccess gets in because it's live in production, with real customers, and that can be verified by visiting the site. A project that only exists as screenshots of something no longer online carries less weight, not because the work wasn't real, but because there's no way for anyone to confirm it on their own.

**An honest lesson, not just an achievement.** I prefer a case that includes what went wrong and what got done about it — like the header misclick rate I documented unresolved in the Mexx case — over one that only tells wins. A portfolio that never shows a limit reads as incomplete, not as perfect.

## What I leave out, even if it took a lot of work

**Projects where I made no relevant decision.** Work executed following someone else's exact instructions, with no room for my own judgement, doesn't demonstrate what a portfolio needs to demonstrate, even if the work itself was solid.

**Projects that no longer reflect how I work today.** Something from several years ago, done with judgement I wouldn't apply anymore, subtracts more than it adds — it suggests that's still my current level, when it isn't.

**Projects redundant with one already included.** If two cases demonstrate exactly the same type of decision, one of the two is excess. I'd rather every project in the portfolio show a different facet — research process, technical architecture, business decision — than repeat the same strength three times.

## The mistake I've seen most often in other people's portfolios

Including every project ever done, in chronological order, with no curation at all. That turns the portfolio into a résumé with pictures, not an argument. The person looking at it has to do the work of finding what's important among ten similar-looking projects, when that selection work should have been done by whoever built the portfolio.

## How I decide the order, once I've chosen what gets in

The strongest project goes first, not the most recent one. Whoever looks at a portfolio rarely makes it to the end, so chronological order — which prioritizes the last thing I did — almost never matches the order that best communicates my judgement.

## The final question before publishing any version of the portfolio

If someone looks at only the first two projects and leaves, do they already have a clear, correct idea of what kind of work I do? If the answer is no, the problem isn't that more projects are missing — it's that the first two aren't choosing well what they need to communicate.
