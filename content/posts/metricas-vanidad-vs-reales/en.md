Any metric that can only go up, that can never drop in a way that reveals a problem, is suspect as a vanity metric. Total registered users is the classic example: that number never goes down, not even when the product is failing, because almost nobody actively "unregisters". A number that can't reflect a real problem can't help you detect one.

## Why vanity metrics are tempting anyway

They look great on a slide, grow over time almost effortlessly, and give a sense of progress even when the real business isn't improving. That's exactly the danger: they give a false sense that everything's fine while the real problem — people who sign up and never come back, for instance — stays invisible behind the number that keeps growing.

## The ones I track on a small SaaS like GymSmartAccess

**30-day retention, not total users.** Out of every ten gyms that start using the system, how many are still actively using it a month later? This number can drop, and when it does, it says something real: that the product isn't sustaining the value it promised on first contact.

**Time to first real value, not time to sign-up.** I don't care how long it takes someone to create an account. I care how long it takes them to reach the moment the product proves its value — in this case, the first automated payment processed without the gym owner having to chase anyone. That's the number that connects directly to [well-designed onboarding](/en/blog/the-first-minute-that-decides-if-someone-comes-back).

**Revenue per active customer, not customer count.** Ten customers paying little and ten customers paying more look the same in a simple "customer count", but they represent completely different businesses. I track actual revenue generated, not how many logos I can show.

**Reason for cancellation, not just churn rate.** How many leave matters less than why they leave. A customer who cancels because they closed their gym is a different signal than one who cancels because the product didn't deliver what it promised. Treating both as the same data point hides exactly the information most needed to improve.

## What I decided not to track, on purpose

**Total visits to the marketing site.** A traffic spike with no associated conversion means nothing for the business — it could be people who arrived by mistake, a bot, or interest with no real buying intent. I track conversion rates from that traffic, not the traffic itself.

**Number of features shipped.** This is a common internal mistake: measuring your own effort as if it were the result. Shipping ten features nobody uses isn't progress, even though it feels like progress from inside the team that built them.

## Why real metrics hurt more to look at

Vanity metrics almost always go up. Real metrics sometimes go down, and they go down exactly when something's wrong — which is exactly when it's most uncomfortable to look at them, and exactly when it's most necessary to. Choosing to track a number that could give you bad news, instead of one that only confirms what you already wanted to believe, is the underlying decision behind all of this.

## The question I apply to any new metric before I start tracking it

**Could this metric go down in a way that tells me something I need to know?** If the answer is no — if the number can only go up or stay flat, regardless of what's happening in the real business — it's a vanity metric, no matter how impressive it looks in a report.
