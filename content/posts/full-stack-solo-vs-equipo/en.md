I'm a full stack developer who works alone on most of my projects, so anything I write here has an obvious bias. That's why this article isn't an argument for one option — it's the objective criteria I use, and one I'd give equally to someone evaluating me or to someone evaluating hiring a ten-person team.

## When a single full stack developer makes sense

**The project's scope is something one person can hold entirely in their head.** This isn't about lines of code, it's about conceptual complexity: if a single person can understand from memory how every part of the system connects, a large team adds coordination without adding real capacity.

**Decision speed matters more than parallel capacity.** A solo developer doesn't have to wait for a sync meeting to decide an architecture change. That's a real advantage when the project needs to iterate fast on feedback, as in the early stage of an MVP.

**The budget doesn't stretch to a team, but does stretch to doing it well with one person.** This sounds obvious but it's worth saying: the alternative to a full stack developer isn't always a team, sometimes it's not doing the project at all. There, one competent developer beats nothing, not a hypothetical team that can't be afforded.

**Design and development need to live in the same head.** When a product's competitive edge depends on the interface and the implementation being thought through together — as I described in [from Figma to production](/en/blog/from-figma-to-production-without-losing-anything) — having both disciplines in one person removes an entire layer of translation that a team with separate roles does have to cross.

## When a team is clearly needed

**The project needs to advance in parallel on fronts that don't depend on each other.** If the frontend, the backend and the data infrastructure all need to be built at the same time because the timeline doesn't allow doing it sequentially, a solo developer there is a genuine bottleneck, not a stylistic choice.

**Specific depth is needed in more than one area at once.** A full stack developer has good general judgement across several layers, but doesn't replace someone who spent ten years optimizing databases at scale, if the project specifically needs that depth from day one.

**The business can't depend on a single person.** If the product needs guaranteed continuity — 24/7 support, someone available even if the lead developer gets sick or goes on vacation — a single point of failure is a real business risk, not a work-style preference.

**The project is going to grow into a team soon anyway.** If three more developers are joining in six months, starting with processes, documentation and conventions built for a team from the start saves the restructuring involved in going from "it all lives in my head" to "this has to be written down so someone else understands it".

## The mistake I see from both sides

From the hiring side: assuming a large team is automatically less risky. A badly coordinated team, with communication failing between roles, can produce a worse result than one competent person — coordination isn't free, it has its own cost and its own risk of failing.

From the side of someone selling themselves as a solo full stack developer: not recognizing when the client's project genuinely needs a team. Saying yes to everything, including what one developer can't sustain well, is the fastest way for the whole project to go wrong and for trust to be lost.

## The question that replaces the debate

Instead of "solo full stack or a team?", the useful question is: **does this specific project carry more risk in coordinating between people, or more risk in not having enough hands?** The answer to that question, not a general preference, is what decides which structure is right.
