A [UX case study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/portfolio-case-study/) well written for another designer can be completely unreadable to the HR person who actually decides whether you get called for a first interview. That isn't the reader's problem. It's the problem of having written for one audience when there are actually two completely different ones reading the same document.

## The two audiences, and what each one needs

A designer or someone technical evaluating your case wants to see the rigor of the process: what method you used, how you interpreted the data, whether your reasoning holds up. An HR recruiter, a non-technical hiring manager, or a client evaluating whether to hire you doesn't have the vocabulary to evaluate the method. But they can perfectly well evaluate whether you solved a real problem and whether you communicate clearly.

Writing only for the first audience loses the second. Writing only for the second loses credibility with the first. It's the first audience that can tell when a case study sounds good but has no real substance behind it.

## How I solve this without sacrificing either one

**Every technical term gets its translation immediately, not in a separate glossary.** I don't write "I ran a [tree test](/en/blog/tree-testing-information-architecture)" and move on. I write "I ran a tree test — a test where someone navigates only the category structure, with no design, to see if the names make sense — and found that...". The technical reader recognizes the method by name. The non-technical reader understands what was done without needing to know the term.

**The result gets told before the method, not after.** The first sentence of every section says what got achieved, in business or user terms. The method that produced it comes afterwards, for whoever wants to go deeper. This reverses the natural academic order — method, then result. Most readers decide whether to keep reading based on the first sentence, so that first sentence has to be understandable to anyone.

**Numbers get explained in their meaning, not just their value.** "40% directness" tells someone with no UX research context nothing. "Only 4 out of 10 people found the category without getting lost, because the name didn't match what they were searching for" says the same thing, with the same precise number, but understandable with no prior knowledge.

## The same finding, told at two levels

Here's what that difference looks like in practice.

Version for someone who knows the vocabulary: "The tree test revealed 40% directness in the Software category; I renamed it to Digital Licenses."

Version I add for any reader: "I asked ten people to find a Windows license in the site's structure, with no design shown to them. Only four out of ten found it without getting lost, because they were searching for 'licenses' and the category was called 'Software'. I renamed it, and the problem disappeared."

The second version isn't a simplification that loses rigor. It tells exactly the same finding, with the same data. It just doesn't assume the reader already knows what a tree test is.

## Why this matters more for my specific profile

Combining development and UX/UI design means my case studies sometimes get read by someone evaluating the technical side and sometimes by someone evaluating the design side. Often neither one fully masters the other. If the case study only speaks one discipline's language, half of my value — the combination of both — stays invisible to whoever doesn't master that specific discipline.

## The test I apply before publishing any UX case study

I give it to someone with no design or development background to read and ask what they understood. If they can explain back to me, in their own words, what the problem was and how it got solved, the case is well written. If they get lost in the jargon halfway through, I still have translation work to do, no matter how rigorous the process I'm describing is.
