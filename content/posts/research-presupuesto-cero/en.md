"We don't have budget for research" is the most common reason to skip investigation entirely. It rests on a false premise: that researching requires paying for participants and premium tools. User research without budget loses precision, it doesn't lose the ability to exist.

## What changes without budget, and what doesn't

Without budget to professionally recruit participants, the sample will be less controlled. Probably people from your close circle, or existing users willing to give a few free minutes. That introduces a real bias: your circle resembles you more than it resembles your target user.

What doesn't change is whether the questions are well written. [A survey with loaded questions](/en/blog/writing-unbiased-survey-questions) is still bad even if nobody was paid to answer it. And a good open question still reveals real information when you ask an acquaintance.

## Methods for user research without budget

**Interviews with existing users, in exchange for nothing more than genuine attention.** If you already have real users, even few, writing to ask for 15 minutes usually works better than expected. People who use your product have opinions they want to share. Often the obstacle isn't cost: it's never having asked.

**Card sorting and tree testing with free-tier tools.** Tools like Optimal Workshop have free plans with participant limits. They cover an exploratory study of 8 to 10 people, the number I [documented as sufficient](/en/blog/tree-testing-information-architecture) for most qualitative studies. [Nielsen measured it decades ago](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/).

**Guerrilla testing, in person, with whoever is available.** Showing a prototype to someone at a coffee shop, or to a family member with no experience of the product, and watching where they hesitate, reveals real problems. It isn't a substitute for a professionally moderated test. But it catches most of the obvious problems, which are the most costly if they reach production.

**Analytics you already have, read as research.** Behavioural data already being collected, where people abandon a form or which pages bounce most, is real, free evidence. It doesn't replace the "why", but it points at where to look first.

## What gets lost, and when that matters

With an unrepresentative sample, qualitative findings stay reasonably reliable: what problems exist, how people describe a task. What becomes unreliable are the numbers. "80% of users prefer X" over five acquaintances means nothing, even though it sounds precise.

The honest way to communicate this is not faking precision that doesn't exist. I say "in the conversations I had, most people mentioned..." instead of a percentage that sounds more rigorous than the sample allows.

## When this limitation starts to genuinely hurt

If the project has enough at stake, a major business decision or a costly redesign, the cost of researching poorly outweighs the cost of doing it properly. That's where it's worth arguing for paid research. Not as a general principle, but as a decision justified by what's at stake.

## The rule I apply

Researching on zero budget beats not researching, almost always. What I never do is present limited research as if it had the rigor of fully funded research. Honesty about the sample's limitations separates modest-but-useful research from research that deceives with false precision.

## What I always write down, however small the study

Three things: how many people I asked, how I reached them, and what I couldn't cover. It takes two lines at the top of the report and completely changes how everything after it reads.

Without that header, any finding reads as if it applied to every user. With it, whoever reads it can calibrate how much weight to give each conclusion.

It also protects the research later. Six months on, nobody remembers whether a finding came from ten interviews or from two conversations in a hallway, and the number that stays in everyone's head is whichever one sounded most convincing at the time.
