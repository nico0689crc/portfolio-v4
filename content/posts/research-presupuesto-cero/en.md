"We don't have budget for research" is the most common reason to skip investigation entirely, and it rests on an incorrect premise: that researching requires paying for participants and premium tools. You lose precision without that budget, you don't lose the ability to research.

## What changes without budget, and what doesn't

Without budget to professionally recruit representative participants, the sample will be less controlled — probably people from your close circle, or existing users willing to give a few free minutes. That introduces a real bias: your close circle probably resembles you more than it resembles your target user.

What doesn't change is the validity of well-written versus badly written questions — [a survey with loaded questions](/en/blog/writing-unbiased-survey-questions) is still bad even if you didn't pay anyone to answer it, and a good open question still reveals real information whether you ask it of an acquaintance or a professionally recruited participant.

## The methods that do work with no budget

**Interviews with existing users, in exchange for nothing more than genuine attention.** If you already have real users, even few, writing to ask for 15 minutes of conversation usually works better than expected — people who already use your product have opinions they want to share, and often the obstacle isn't cost, it's simply never having asked.

**Card sorting and tree testing with free-tier tools.** Tools like Optimal Workshop have free plans with participant limits, enough for a first exploratory study with 8 to 10 people — which is exactly the number I [documented as sufficient](/en/blog/tree-testing-information-architecture) for most qualitative studies.

**Guerrilla testing, in person, with whoever is available.** Showing a prototype on your phone to someone at a coffee shop, or to a family member with no prior experience of the product, and observing where they hesitate, still reveals real usability problems. It isn't a perfect substitute for a professionally moderated test, but it catches most of the obvious problems, which also tend to be the most costly if they reach production uncorrected.

**Analytics you already have, read as research.** Behavioural data already being collected — where people abandon a form, which pages have the highest bounce rate — is real, free evidence of behaviour, as long as the instrumentation already exists. It doesn't replace the "why", but it clearly points to the "where" to look first.

## What gets lost, and when that matters

With an unrepresentative sample, qualitative findings — what problems exist, how people describe a task — remain reasonably reliable. What becomes far less reliable are numbers that look quantitative: "80% of users prefer X" over a sample of five acquaintances means nothing, even though the number sounds precise.

The honest way to communicate this is not faking precision that doesn't exist. I say "in the conversations I had, most people mentioned..." instead of a percentage that sounds more rigorous than the sample allows.

## When this limitation starts to genuinely hurt

If the project has enough at stake — a major business decision, a costly redesign — the cost of researching poorly starts to outweigh the cost of researching with real budget. That's where it's worth arguing for investing in paid research, not as a general principle but as a specific decision justified by what's at stake in that particular project.

## The rule I apply

Researching on zero budget beats not researching, almost always. What I never do is present the results of limited research as if they had the rigor of fully funded research — honesty about the sample's limitations is what separates modest-but-useful research from research that deceives with false precision.
